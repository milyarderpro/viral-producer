# Viral Trivia Content DNA

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the editorial DNA for short Facebook Reels trivia scripts aimed primarily at audiences in the United States, with Canada, the United Kingdom, and Australia as secondary markets.

The reference dataset is a style reference only. It is not an approved fact source, and its wording must not be copied. Every production fact must be independently researched, verified, and checked against the fact ledger before use.

## 2. Core Content Promise

Every post should deliver six compact, surprising, easy-to-understand facts that make viewers want to pause, replay, remember, share, or tell someone else.

The basic content formula is:

Familiar subject + unexpected truth + concrete detail

Each fact should create a clear mental reaction such as:

- "I did not know that."
- "That sounds impossible."
- "I need to tell someone."
- "I have seen this before but never understood it."
- "That is useful enough to remember."

The content must never sacrifice accuracy or safety to create a stronger reaction.

## 3. Audience

### Primary market

United States.

### Secondary markets

- Canada
- United Kingdom
- Australia

### Language

Use simple, conversational American English that remains easy to understand across all four markets.

Language should be:

- natural rather than academic;
- understandable without specialist knowledge;
- culturally neutral when possible;
- concrete rather than abstract;
- suitable for fast on-screen reading;
- free of unnecessary jargon, slang, and regional idioms.

Explain unfamiliar local terms inside the sentence when they are essential.

## 4. Country Distribution

When producing country-specific trivia without a country requested by the user, use this long-term distribution:

- United States: 60%
- Canada: 15%
- United Kingdom: 15%
- Australia: 10%

Global topics such as animals, the human body, food, and everyday science do not need an artificial country connection.

Do not select the same country focus for more than two consecutive country-specific posts unless the user explicitly requests it.

## 5. Default Topic Distribution

Use the following long-term topic mix:

- Geography and history: 30%
- Animals and nature: 20%
- Human body and everyday science: 15%
- Food and household knowledge: 15%
- Inventions, firsts, and unusual records: 15%
- Safe practical knowledge: 5%

Topic selection should favor rotation. Do not repeat the same topic in consecutive posts unless requested by the user or required for a named series.

### Geography and history

Preferred angles:

- unexpected borders or locations;
- overlooked historical events;
- unusual city or state features;
- surprising origins;
- verified national or regional distinctions;
- historical practices with clear context.

Avoid political persuasion, current partisan disputes, unsupported legends presented as fact, and claims that depend on outdated law or policy.

### Animals and nature

Preferred angles:

- anatomy;
- unusual behavior;
- adaptations;
- sensory abilities;
- biological classifications;
- surprising comparisons.

Avoid animal myths, exaggerated abilities, and human-like interpretations unsupported by research.

### Human body and everyday science

Preferred angles:

- perception;
- anatomy;
- common biological processes;
- everyday chemistry or physics;
- counterintuitive scientific explanations.

Avoid diagnosis, treatment, medical instructions, and claims likely to cause health anxiety.

### Food and household knowledge

Preferred angles:

- storage behavior;
- food science;
- material properties;
- why common household effects occur;
- safe and verified maintenance advice.

Avoid unsafe cleaning mixtures, questionable stain-removal hacks, food-safety shortcuts, and advice that could damage property.

### Inventions, firsts, and unusual records

Preferred angles:

- original purposes;
- unexpected inventors;
- first documented uses;
- historical design choices;
- unusual verified records.

Record claims must be checked for current accuracy and must retain qualifiers such as "documented," "known," or "one of" when required.

### Safe practical knowledge

Preferred angles:

- low-risk organization;
- travel preparation;
- observation;
- material care;
- non-medical everyday efficiency.

Do not provide wilderness survival, water purification, fire-starting, first-aid, medical, legal, or emergency advice unless the user explicitly requests it and authoritative safety guidance is available.

## 6. Standard Post Format

Every normal post must use this structure:

    Did you know?

    [Fact 1]
    [Fact 2]
    [Fact 3]
    [Fact 4]
    [Fact 5]
    [Fact 6]

    Enjoyed these facts? Like the video and follow for more!

Formatting rules:

- Use exactly six facts unless the user explicitly requests another number.
- Put one fact on each paragraph or visual line group.
- Do not use bullets or numbering in the final copy.
- Do not show citations inside the final copy.
- Do not add a title between the hook and the facts.
- Do not add hashtags, emojis, commentary, or production notes inside the copy block.
- Keep the hook and CTA identical by default for series recognition.
- Do not end fact lines with periods unless punctuation is required for clarity.
- Store sources and audit information separately from the copy.

## 7. Fact Length and Visual Balance

Dataset baseline:

- 61 facts across 10 reference posts;
- six facts in nine posts and seven facts in one post;
- average length of approximately 14.3 English words;
- observed range of 11 to 18 words.

Production target:

- ideal: 12 to 15 words per fact;
- acceptable: 11 to 18 words;
- one complete idea per fact;
- similar visual width across all six facts.

A shorter sentence is preferable to adding filler solely to reach a word count.

Do not compress a fact so aggressively that it loses a necessary qualifier, date, location, limitation, or safety condition.

## 8. Sentence Construction

Each fact should contain three components when possible:

1. A familiar anchor.
2. A clear surprise or reversal.
3. A concrete detail, consequence, number, comparison, place, or time.

Preferred structures include:

- "[Subject] actually [unexpected property or behavior]"
- "[Subject] cannot [assumed ability] because [brief reason]"
- "[Place] is the only [verified distinction] in [clear scope]"
- "Before [familiar development], people [unexpected practice]"
- "A single [familiar object] contains enough [property] to [concrete comparison]"
- "[Common item] behaves differently when [specific condition]"

Do not force every sentence into the same grammatical opening. Maintain visual consistency without making the copy sound mechanical.

## 9. Surprise Operators

Use one strong surprise mechanism per fact:

- only or unique distinction;
- first documented occurrence;
- cannot or never under a defined condition;
- counterintuitive classification;
- exact number or ratio;
- surprising historical origin;
- familiar item with an unfamiliar function;
- scale comparison;
- everyday mistake and consequence;
- unusual biological feature;
- visually strange but verified behavior.

Use absolute words carefully. Terms such as "only," "first," "never," "always," "largest," and "oldest" require direct source support and a clearly defined scope.

Limit repetitive trigger words such as "actually," "only," and "first" to approximately two or three uses per post.

## 10. Emotional Mix

A strong post should not deliver six facts with the same emotional flavor.

Aim for a balanced selection such as:

- two high-surprise facts;
- one fact with a memorable number or scale;
- one familiar everyday connection;
- one visually strange, funny, or mildly gross fact;
- one highly shareable closing fact.

Mild gross-out content is acceptable when non-graphic and scientifically relevant. Do not use gore, suffering, humiliation, or shock for its own sake.

## 11. Fact Ordering

Order facts for retention and looping:

1. Strongest scroll-stopping fact.
2. Clear counterintuitive fact.
3. Fact with a number, scale, or concrete comparison.
4. Familiar or practically relevant fact.
5. Visually strange or socially shareable fact.
6. Second-strongest fact, chosen to create a satisfying final payoff before the video loops.

Do not bury the strongest facts in the middle.

## 12. Topic Coherence

Each post should have one recognizable theme.

Good:

- six strange animal adaptations;
- six surprising facts about Canada;
- six things commonly stored incorrectly;
- six inventions created for unexpected reasons.

Weak:

- one animal fact, one political fact, one cleaning hack, one medical claim, one city fact, and one unrelated record.

A mixed "random facts" post is allowed occasionally, but themed posts are the default because they are easier to title, recognize, save, and serialize.

## 13. Research and Accuracy Standard

Research the underlying claim before writing viral copy.

Minimum requirements:

- use at least one authoritative or primary source for ordinary low-risk facts;
- use stronger corroboration for changing, disputed, medical, safety, or record-based claims;
- record the source URL and verification date outside the final copy;
- preserve important qualifiers from the source;
- discard a claim when its meaning cannot be verified clearly;
- prefer a less dramatic accurate claim over a more dramatic ambiguous one.

Preferred source order:

1. Government agencies and official public institutions.
2. Universities, museums, scientific organizations, and peer-reviewed research.
3. Official organizations responsible for the subject or record.
4. High-quality reference works and reputable reporting for historical context.

Do not use another trivia page, social post, short-form video, AI-generated answer, or unsourced listicle as the final authority.

### Time-sensitive claims

Recheck claims involving:

- current law or government policy;
- active records;
- population;
- prices or monetary comparisons;
- officeholders;
- company statistics;
- scientific estimates that may have been updated;
- words such as current, largest, oldest, first, or only.

Include a date or scope when the claim could become outdated.

## 14. Accuracy-Preserving Copy Rules

Never:

- turn "may" into "does";
- turn "one of the largest" into "the largest";
- turn an estimate into an exact measurement;
- remove a location or historical period that defines the claim;
- present folklore, rumor, or legend as established fact;
- invent a cause merely because two facts are associated;
- attach an impressive number from a different source or context;
- use a technically true statement that creates a materially false impression.

If a necessary qualifier makes a line too long, replace the fact instead of deleting the qualifier.

## 15. Originality and Duplicate Prevention

The reference dataset must not be copied sentence-for-sentence.

For every production fact:

- express the verified claim in original language;
- create a canonical claim before writing the surface copy;
- assign a semantic claim signature;
- check the appropriate fact ledger;
- reject the candidate if the underlying claim has already been reserved or published;
- treat paraphrases, translated versions, reordered wording, and changed numbers referring to the same underlying relationship as duplicates.

Example:

    polar_bear|skin_color|black

The following are duplicates of the same claim:

- Polar bears have black skin.
- The skin beneath a polar bear's fur is black.
- Under their light-colored fur, polar bears have dark skin.

A new angle is not automatically a new fact. It must communicate a genuinely different relationship or verified property.

## 16. Safety and Trust Boundaries

Reject or escalate content involving:

- medical diagnosis or treatment;
- instructions for consuming uncertain substances;
- unsafe water purification;
- dangerous fire-starting methods;
- emergency rescue claims without official guidance;
- chemical combinations;
- illegal border crossing or trespassing;
- instructions that facilitate harm;
- stereotypes about protected groups;
- current political misinformation;
- allegations about living people;
- sensational claims likely to create unnecessary fear.

A surprising fact is not worth publishing if it could reasonably cause harm or materially mislead the audience.

## 17. Quality Score

Score every draft from 0 to 2 on each dimension:

1. Opening strength.
2. Surprise quality across all six facts.
3. Concrete detail and memorability.
4. Shareability or save value.
5. Readability and visual balance.
6. Factual confidence and source quality.

Maximum score: 12.

Publishing threshold:

- total score must be at least 10;
- readability must score 2;
- factual confidence must score 2;
- every hard formatting, safety, and duplicate rule must pass.

A high total score cannot compensate for an unverified claim, a duplicate fact, or a safety failure.

## 18. Final Editorial Checklist

Before a draft may be saved as ready for approval, confirm:

- The post has exactly six facts.
- The hook and CTA follow the standard format.
- Each fact communicates one complete idea.
- Each fact is ideally 12 to 15 words and no more than 18 words.
- The six lines have reasonably balanced visual length.
- The topic is coherent.
- Fact 1 and Fact 6 are among the strongest.
- The emotional mix is not repetitive.
- Every claim has been independently verified.
- Required qualifiers remain intact.
- No fact is duplicated in the current batch or fact ledger.
- No unsafe or high-risk instruction is present.
- The final copy contains no citations, bullets, hashtags, emojis, or audit notes.
- The quality score is at least 10 out of 12.
